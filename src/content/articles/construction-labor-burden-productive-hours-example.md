An employee’s hourly wage is only one input to a construction labor estimate. The estimate also depends on employment costs, which hours are productive and which costs are recovered elsewhere in the price. A single multiplier hides those decisions.

Build a rate that another person can reproduce. This guide uses invented numbers to demonstrate the arithmetic. It does not prescribe a tax rate, insurance rate, wage or markup for your company.

## Decide what the rate is supposed to measure

Three useful rates answer different questions. The wage rate describes employee pay. A burdened labor rate adds applicable employment costs. A selling or billing rate also needs to account for the pricing approach, overhead recovery and profit objectives.

Do not compare these rates as if they are interchangeable. A quoted subcontract labor rate may include supervision or tools that an employee wage does not. A historical labor percentage may use revenue as its denominator while another report uses total job cost. Write the definitions next to the numbers before drawing conclusions.

In a job-costing system, consistency matters across the estimate, time records and actual-cost report. [Procore’s job-costing overview](https://www.procore.com/library/job-costing) describes organizing project costs into categories and more detailed segments. The operating implication is straightforward: a cost cannot be compared reliably with a budget if the two use different categories.

## Build a transparent cost sheet

Start with a defined period and one employee category or crew. Use payroll and accounting records to identify applicable employer costs. Separate known amounts from assumptions and record the source and review date for each input.

The following is an illustrative weekly worksheet:

| Input | Illustrative amount | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paid hours | 40 hours | Time period and pay rules |
| Base wage | $25 per hour | Employee or crew category |
| Wages | $1,000 | 40 × $25 |
| Other employer costs | $300 | Applicable taxes, insurance and benefits |
| Total employment cost | $1,300 | No duplicated costs |
| Productive job hours | 32 hours | Definition and supporting time records |

Dividing $1,300 by all 40 paid hours gives $32.50 per paid hour. Dividing the same amount by 32 productive hours gives approximately $40.63 per productive hour. Neither result is a universal construction rate. They answer different questions using the same fictional cost total.

If an estimate requires 160 productive hours, using $40.625 produces an illustrative employment-cost allowance of $6,500. Using the $25 wage alone would produce $4,000. The difference is not automatically profit; it reflects costs and time assumptions that the wage-only calculation omitted.

## Keep overhead out of the calculation twice

Choose a consistent treatment for supervision, vehicles, small tools and other costs. Some companies assign particular costs directly to jobs. Others recover them through an overhead approach. The danger is counting the same cost in both places or leaving it out of both.

Label the worksheet with the costs included and excluded. For example, if a project supervisor is estimated as a separate direct-cost line, make sure the crew’s rate does not also contain that supervisor’s entire cost. If a rate excludes equipment, include the relevant equipment allowance elsewhere in the estimate.

Reconcile the model to your accounting structure rather than inventing a new set of categories for each bid. The [U.S. Small Business Administration’s financial-management guidance](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/manage-your-business/) is a useful starting point for maintaining the records behind these decisions.

## Test the hours assumption before adjusting the price

Productive hours are not a moral judgment about the crew. A job can lose productive time because of missing materials, access restrictions, unresolved drawings or repeated mobilization. The estimate should describe the work conditions it assumes.

Run a simple sensitivity check. In the example, if the same $1,300 of employment cost supports only 28 productive hours, the rate becomes approximately $46.43. That is a different planning scenario, not evidence that the company actually incurred that result.

Ask the project team which conditions would make the lower-hours scenario likely. Where possible, address those conditions through delivery planning, work sequencing or clearer handoffs. Increasing the selling price may be one response, but the cost sheet also identifies operational questions worth resolving.

## Compare estimates with completed work

After a job or meaningful phase closes, compare estimated hours with recorded hours using the same cost codes and scope. Investigate whether a difference came from quantities, productivity, rework, a scope change or a coding error. One blended percentage cannot explain all five.

Record what should change in the next estimate and what should change in the field workflow. Keep the original estimate available so the comparison remains honest. Updating a forecast is useful; overwriting the original assumption removes the evidence needed to learn from it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What percentage should labor cost on a construction project?

A universal labor percentage would be misleading. Trade, project scope, subcontracting and the denominator all matter. Compare fully burdened labor against the project estimate and comparable completed work, using consistent definitions.

### What is the best job costing software for construction?

The right system must fit your accounting, cost codes, field time capture and purchasing workflow. Test it with one representative job and verify reconciliation, committed costs, exports and permissions before choosing.

### How do you calculate job costing?

Assign labor, materials, equipment, subcontract and other applicable costs to a job and consistent cost codes. Add costs to date to a current remaining-cost forecast, then compare the projected result with the budget.

### How much should I charge for construction labor?

Start with fully burdened labor cost and expected productive hours, then consider overhead, profit and contract terms. A billing rate and an employee’s wage are different numbers; validate the assumptions for your market.

### How do I calculate my labor cost?

Add wages, applicable employer taxes, insurance, benefits and other employment costs for the period. Divide by the relevant productive hours when estimating an effective hourly cost, keeping assumptions consistent between jobs.

### How much does a $20 an hour employee cost an employer?

More than wages alone, but there is no universal multiplier. For illustration, $800 in wages plus $240 in employer costs over 40 hours equals $26 per hour before any additional overhead allocation.

### What is the formula for calculating labor cost?

For a planning rate, divide total applicable labor cost by productive hours. For a task estimate, multiply expected productive hours by that rate. Include overtime and other job-specific conditions where they apply.

### How do you calculate construction costs?

Define the scope and quantities, estimate labor, materials, equipment and subcontract work, then include applicable indirect costs and documented risk allowances. Keep estimate, committed costs, actual costs and remaining forecast distinguishable.
